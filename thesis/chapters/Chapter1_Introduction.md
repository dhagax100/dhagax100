# CHAPTER 1
# INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 Background

Solar radiation is the prime source for most renewable energy systems and the most abundant energy source reaching Earth. The continuous growth of industrial activity, urban development, and commercial demand has placed increasing pressure on global energy systems. For many decades, this demand has been met largely through fossil-fuel-based resources such as coal, oil, and natural gas. Although these resources have supported economic and industrial development, their large-scale consumption has also intensified environmental problems through the release of carbon dioxide and other atmospheric pollutants. These emissions are associated with climate change, air-quality deterioration, and adverse impacts on human health. Therefore, reducing dependence on fossil fuels has become both an environmental necessity and a technological challenge.

One of the clearest indicators of this challenge is the continued increase in atmospheric greenhouse gases. Renewable energy resources, including hydropower, biomass, geothermal energy, solar energy, wind energy, and wave and tidal energy, offer important pathways for reducing the environmental impact of energy production. Among these alternatives, solar energy is especially attractive because it is abundant, widely distributed, and capable of producing both heat and electricity. Solar energy represents one of the largest renewable energy resources available to humanity; the sun delivers an estimated 173,000 terawatts of energy continuously to the Earth's surface, far exceeding current global energy consumption. These figures show that the main challenge is not the availability of solar energy itself, but the efficiency, reliability, and cost-effectiveness of the technologies used to collect and convert it.

Solar energy technologies may be classified according to the type of system and the form of useful energy produced. In general, they can be active or passive systems. Passive systems rely mainly on natural collection and heat-transfer mechanisms, while active systems require external components such as pumps, tracking mechanisms, or electronic control units. Solar technologies may also be grouped according to the useful output they provide: photovoltaic systems convert sunlight directly into electricity, whereas solar thermal systems absorb solar radiation and convert it into useful heat.

Each solar-conversion route has its own advantages and limitations. Photovoltaic systems have developed rapidly and their costs have decreased over time; however, the conversion efficiency of conventional photovoltaic cells is often limited, and their performance may decline when cell temperature increases. Solar thermal technologies, in contrast, are particularly suitable for applications where useful heat is required, including water heating, cooling, desalination, industrial process heating, and power generation through thermodynamic cycles. In solar thermal systems, absorbed radiation is transferred as heat to a working fluid such as water, thermal oil, molten salt, air, nitrogen, or helium. Concentrating solar thermal collectors are particularly important because they can achieve higher operating temperatures than non-concentrating collectors by focusing direct solar radiation onto a smaller receiver area. Among these technologies, parabolic trough solar collectors have received significant attention because of their technical maturity and suitability for medium- and high-temperature applications. Improving their thermal performance remains essential for increasing useful energy output, reducing losses, and supporting wider deployment of solar thermal energy systems.

## 1.2 Solar Collectors

Solar collectors are thermal devices used to capture solar radiation and convert it into useful heat. The collected energy is usually transferred to a working fluid, which then delivers the heat to a process, a storage unit, or a power-generation cycle. The choice of collector depends mainly on the required operating temperature, the concentration level, the tracking method, and the final application. For this reason, solar collectors are commonly classified into stationary collectors and sun-tracking concentrating collectors.

Stationary collectors, also known as non-tracking collectors, absorb solar radiation without continuously following the apparent motion of the Sun. This category includes flat-plate collectors, evacuated-tube collectors, compound parabolic collectors, bowl collectors, through-pass air collectors, and unglazed transpired collectors. These collectors are generally simple, mechanically reliable, and suitable for low- and medium-temperature applications such as water heating, space heating, crop drying, and air heating. Their main limitation is that they do not strongly concentrate solar radiation, which restricts their ability to reach high operating temperatures.

Sun-tracking concentrating collectors are designed to increase the solar flux incident on the receiver by reflecting or refracting radiation from a relatively large aperture area onto a smaller receiver area using mirrors or lenses. One-axis tracking collectors include parabolic trough collectors, cylindrical trough collectors, and linear Fresnel collectors, while two-axis tracking collectors include parabolic dish collectors, solar tower systems, and circular Fresnel systems.

A key parameter in concentrating solar collectors is the concentration ratio, commonly defined as the ratio between the concentrator aperture area and the receiver area:

```
CR = Ac / Ar                                                        (1.1)
```

where CR is the concentration ratio, Ac is the concentrator aperture area, and Ar is the receiver area. A concentration ratio greater than one indicates that solar radiation is being concentrated. Higher concentration ratios allow the collector to reach higher temperatures, but they also increase the need for accurate optical alignment and tracking.

In industrial solar thermal applications, concentrating solar collectors are mainly represented by four configurations: parabolic trough solar collectors, linear Fresnel reflectors, solar towers, and parabolic dish collectors. Parabolic trough and linear Fresnel systems are line-focus, single-axis-tracking technologies suited to medium concentration and medium-to-high temperatures; solar towers and parabolic dishes are point-focus, two-axis-tracking technologies capable of much higher concentration and temperature, at the cost of greater system complexity. Among these, parabolic trough solar collectors have become one of the most established concentrating solar technologies. Their single-axis tracking arrangement is less complex than the two-axis tracking required in solar towers and parabolic dish systems, making PTSCs suitable for applications such as industrial process heat, solar refrigeration, air-conditioning, steam generation, and solar thermal power plants. Parabolic trough technology has historically held the largest share of the global concentrating solar thermal market, which makes it a practical platform for continued research and development. However, PTSC performance still depends strongly on receiver heat transfer, heat-loss reduction, and the thermophysical behavior of the working fluid — the aspects that motivate the present thesis.

## 1.3 Parabolic Trough Solar Collector (PTSC)

A parabolic trough solar collector (PTSC) is a line-focus concentrating solar collector that uses a curved reflective surface to concentrate direct solar radiation onto a receiver tube located along the focal line of the parabola. The concentrated radiation is absorbed by the receiver and transferred as heat to a working fluid flowing inside the absorber tube. At its core, a PTSC operates by bringing together two distinct physical processes: the optical concentration of sunlight and the convective removal of the resulting heat. The curved reflector focuses incoming direct beam radiation onto the receiver, where a selective absorber coating converts the concentrated flux into thermal energy at the tube wall; this heat is then transferred through the absorber wall to the heat transfer fluid (HTF), which transports the useful energy onward.

A PTSC consists mainly of a reflector, supporting structure, receiver, working fluid, and tracking system. The reflector must maintain high reflectance and accurate parabolic geometry. The supporting structure must preserve alignment between the mirror and receiver under self-weight, wind, and operating loads. The receiver must absorb the concentrated radiation while reducing thermal losses, typically through a metallic absorber tube combined with an outer glass envelope and an evacuated annular gap that suppresses convective loss from the hot absorber to the cooler glass wall. The working fluid must remove heat effectively from the absorber tube. Finally, the tracking system must keep the collector aligned with the Sun so that radiation remains concentrated near the focal line.

The heat-transfer process inside the receiver is not uniform. Part of the absorbed energy is transferred to the HTF through convection at the inner wall of the absorber tube, while the remaining portion can be lost through radiation and convection toward the glass cover and surroundings. This makes receiver thermal management a central issue in PTSC design: non-uniform circumferential solar-flux distribution produces absorber wall-temperature gradients, intensifies radiative losses, and introduces mechanical stress at the glass-to-metal seal. These limitations explain why recent PTSC research has focused strongly on receiver improvement, advanced working fluids, and internal heat-transfer enhancement — the three directions brought together in this thesis.

**Receiver geometry adopted in this study.** The present work is based on the LS-2 parabolic trough collector geometry, with an absorber (receiver) tube of length 4000 mm, inner diameter 66 mm, outer diameter 70 mm (2 mm wall thickness), and an aperture area of 39.2 m². To enhance internal convective heat transfer, the receiver is fitted with a central hollow twisted square promoter (HTSP) made of stainless steel and running the full length of the tube. Unlike a solid surface-modifying insert such as a wavy promoter or a delta-winglet vortex generator, the hollow twisted square creates two connected fluid passages within the receiver: an annular region between the absorber wall and the outer promoter surface, and an interior channel through the hollow square core. This dual-passage arrangement alters the velocity distribution, generates a distinct swirl pattern as the square cross-section twists along the tube axis, and modifies the thermal boundary layer in a way that a single solid insert cannot replicate. The axial twist pitch of the promoter, together with its cross-sectional size and wall thickness, is varied systematically in this study, introducing geometric degrees of freedom that are evaluated jointly with the working-fluid properties described in Section 1.4.

## 1.4 Working Fluids

The working fluid is one of the most important elements in a parabolic trough solar collector because it receives the thermal energy absorbed by the receiver tube and transports it to the storage unit, heat exchanger, steam generator, or direct thermal process. In PTSC literature, the working fluid is commonly referred to as the heat transfer fluid (HTF). Its selection affects the useful heat gain, absorber temperature, pressure drop, pumping power, operating temperature range, and overall thermal efficiency of the system. Therefore, improving the working fluid is not a secondary design issue, but a direct route for improving PTSC performance.

The suitability of a HTF depends on several thermophysical and operational properties: adequate thermal conductivity and specific heat for effective energy absorption, manageable viscosity to keep pumping demands reasonable, resistance to thermal breakdown at operating temperatures, chemical compatibility with receiver materials, and acceptable cost for large-scale deployment. Conventional working fluids used in PTSC systems include water or steam, synthetic thermal oils, molten salts, gases, and, in some research cases, liquid metals. Each category carries its own trade-offs: water-steam systems require high pressure and face scaling and phase-change challenges at elevated temperature; synthetic thermal oils remain liquid-phase at required operating temperatures without the elevated pressure water demands, but are limited by thermal degradation above their rated temperature ceiling (for example, Syltherm 800, the base fluid adopted in this study, is a silicone-based heat transfer fluid widely used in commercial PTSC installations); molten salts extend the usable temperature range and suit thermal storage but require freeze protection due to their high melting point; gaseous HTFs avoid freezing problems but have lower density and heat capacity than liquid HTFs.

In recent years, nanofluids have received strong attention as advanced working fluids for solar thermal collectors. Nanofluids are prepared by suspending solid nanoparticles — typically smaller than 100 nm — within a carrier liquid. Because solid materials generally conduct heat far more efficiently than liquids, incorporating them at the nanoscale can improve the effective thermal properties of the resulting mixture, reducing absorber wall temperature and improving useful heat gain when the particles are adequately stabilized and distributed. However, nanofluids also introduce practical limitations: increasing nanoparticle concentration can increase viscosity, raising pressure drop and pumping power, and nanoparticles may agglomerate, settle, or lose stability at high temperature. The best nanofluid is therefore not simply the one with the highest thermal conductivity, but the one that provides a favorable balance between heat-transfer enhancement, pressure-drop penalty, stability, cost, and compatibility with the receiver materials.

Hybrid nanofluids extend this approach further by combining two or more nanoparticle materials in one base fluid, aiming for a better balance of thermal conductivity, optical absorption, stability, and rheological behavior than can be achieved with a single nanoparticle type. The present thesis evaluates three Syltherm-800-based hybrid nanofluids — Fe₂O₃-GO, Al₂O₃-MWCNT, and Ag-MgO — each with brick-shaped nanoparticles of 50 nm diameter, across nanoparticle volume concentrations of 0.5%, 1.0%, and 2.0%. Fe₂O₃-GO provides direct continuity with the advisor's own published baseline study (Mohammed et al., 2022) in the same PTSC configuration; Al₂O₃-MWCNT combines a well-characterized ceramic oxide with multi-walled carbon nanotubes for enhanced heat-capacity behavior; and Ag-MgO pairs a noble metal of very high thermal conductivity with a metal oxide offering thermal stability across the 400-650 K operating range considered in this study.

## 1.5 Heat Transfer Passive Techniques

The central aim of receiver-side heat-transfer enhancement in PTSC systems is to raise the rate at which energy deposited on the absorber wall is carried away by the flowing working fluid. Inside the PTSC receiver, the absorber tube experiences concentrated irradiance that is typically stronger on the lower, focal-side surface than on the upper side. When internal convection is insufficient to dissipate this energy rapidly, the absorber wall temperature rises, radiative and conductive losses escalate, and the circumferential temperature difference introduces mechanical stress. Improving the internal convective heat transfer inside the receiver tube is therefore an important route for increasing useful heat gain and protecting the receiver structure.

Enhancement techniques for internal convective heat transfer are broadly divided into three categories: active, passive, and compound. Active methods depend on an external energy source to disturb the flow. Passive methods achieve the same objective through geometry alone, without drawing on any external power supply, typically through flow disruption via inserts or surface modifications, enhanced lateral mixing across the tube cross-section, thinning of the thermal boundary layer near the heated wall, and, in some configurations, an increase in the wetted surface area available for heat exchange. Compound methods combine two or more enhancement approaches, such as the use of inserts together with nanofluids or modified receiver geometries — the approach adopted in this thesis.

Passive devices commonly studied in PTSC receivers include twisted tape inserts, perforated and porous inserts, corrugated or ribbed absorber tubes, and wavy tapes or promoters. Each raises the convective heat-transfer coefficient at the absorber wall through a distinct mechanism — swirl flow, jet-like mixing, boundary-layer interruption, or localized vortex generation — but each also generates additional flow resistance, translating into a higher friction factor and greater pressure drop along the receiver length. Passive techniques must therefore be judged by their thermal-hydraulic performance rather than by Nusselt number alone. A widely adopted metric for this purpose is the performance evaluation criterion (PEC), which places the thermal gain and the hydraulic cost on a common basis:

```
PEC = (Nu/Nu_s) / (f/f_s)^(1/3)                                     (1.2)
```

where Nu and f are the Nusselt number and friction factor of the enhanced receiver, while Nu_s and f_s refer to the corresponding smooth receiver. A PEC greater than one indicates that the improvement in heat transfer is large enough to offset the additional pumping energy required, confirming that the modification is thermodynamically worthwhile.

The central hollow twisted square promoter (HTSP) investigated in this thesis belongs to this family of passive techniques, but departs from previously studied insert geometries — including the wavy promoter examined in the advisor's own baseline study — by creating two simultaneous flow passages (annulus and interior channel) rather than modifying a single flow path. This distinction, and the resulting research gap it is intended to address, is developed further in Chapter 2 and formalized in the problem statement below.

## 1.6 Problem Statement

Parabolic trough solar collectors are among the most mature concentrating solar technologies, yet their overall performance remains strongly limited by the thermal behavior of the receiver tube. The receiver is exposed to concentrated solar radiation that is not distributed uniformly around the absorber circumference. If the absorbed energy cannot be removed quickly enough by the flowing fluid, the absorber surface temperature climbs, radiation losses to the surroundings intensify, and the circumferential temperature distribution becomes increasingly non-uniform — introducing mechanical stress, shortening receiver service life, and raising the risk of permanent deformation at the glass-to-metal seal.

Conventional heat transfer fluids provide stable operation but limit further improvement: Syltherm 800 and other synthetic thermal oils are widely used for their maturity and compatibility with commercial PTSC systems, but their thermal conductivity and specific heat set a ceiling on achievable heat-transfer coefficient without further intervention. Advanced working fluids — nanofluids and hybrid nanofluids — have been proposed to overcome this limitation, but the resulting improvement in heat transfer is not obtained without cost, since increasing nanoparticle concentration raises viscosity, pressure drop, and pumping power. Passive heat-transfer enhancement techniques face a parallel trade-off: inserts and promoters that disturb the flow and improve mixing also increase flow resistance, so they cannot be selected on the basis of Nusselt-number enhancement alone.

It is important to distinguish the present work from the closely related study of Mohammed et al. (2022), which examined a PTSC receiver equipped with a wavy promoter using the Fe₂O₃-GO/Syltherm 800 hybrid nanofluid, within the same operating range and the same performance-evaluation framework. The present research builds directly on that foundation but investigates a fundamentally different passive geometry: a central hollow twisted square promoter. Unlike a wavy promoter, which applies surface undulations to a solid insert, the hollow twisted square creates two connected fluid passages — an annular region between the absorber wall and the outer promoter surface, and an interior channel through the hollow square core. This dual-passage arrangement alters the velocity distribution, generates a distinct swirl pattern, and modifies the thermal boundary layer in a way that a wavy promoter cannot replicate. In addition, the present study varies the promoter's axial twist pitch, cross-sectional size, and wall thickness systematically — geometric degrees of freedom not explored in the 2022 baseline — and extends the working-fluid comparison beyond a single hybrid nanofluid to three candidate hybrid nanofluids (Fe₂O₃-GO, Al₂O₃-MWCNT, and Ag-MgO) across three nanoparticle volume concentrations.

The central problem addressed in this thesis is therefore that the thermal performance of PTSC receiver tubes remains constrained by insufficient heat removal, non-uniform absorber temperature distribution, and the competing effects of heat-transfer enhancement and hydraulic resistance. A rigorous numerical study is needed — one that simultaneously assesses the thermal, hydraulic, and thermodynamic consequences of combining a central hollow twisted square promoter with hybrid nanofluids, and establishes whether this combined approach delivers a measurable performance gain over the unmodified smooth receiver.

## 1.7 Objectives

The aim of this research is to numerically evaluate whether combining a central hollow twisted square promoter with hybrid-nanofluid working fluids can improve the thermal, hydraulic, and thermodynamic performance of a PTSC receiver tube, compared with a conventional smooth receiver operating with the base thermal oil.

The study scope is a receiver-scale, steady-state, three-dimensional numerical investigation of the PTSC absorber tube under turbulent flow, using ANSYS Fluent with the pressure-based solver, SIMPLE pressure-velocity coupling, PRESTO! pressure discretization, second-order upwind schemes, the realizable k-ε turbulence model with enhanced wall treatment (y+ ≈ 1), and the Discrete Ordinates radiation model. The investigation follows a Taguchi L27 orthogonal-array design spanning five factors at three levels each:

| Factor | Level 1 | Level 2 | Level 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Twist pitch ratio, Pt/dti | 3.03 (200 mm) | 4.55 (300 mm) | 6.06 (400 mm) |
| Promoter size ratio, dpo/dti | 0.23 (15 mm) | 0.38 (25 mm) | 0.53 (35 mm) |
| Wall thickness ratio, tp/dpo | 0.04 (1 mm) | 0.08 (2 mm) | 0.12 (3 mm) |
| Hybrid nanofluid type | Fe₂O₃-GO | Al₂O₃-MWCNT | Ag-MgO |
| Nanoparticle volume concentration | 0.5% | 1.0% | 2.0% |

operated across Reynolds numbers of 5,000-100,000, inlet temperatures of 400-650 K, and a direct normal irradiance of 1000 W/m² with 300 K ambient temperature.

The specific objectives are to:

1. Develop and validate a three-dimensional CFD model of the PTSC receiver with the central hollow twisted square promoter, benchmarked against the Gnielinski and Petukhov smooth-tube correlations, the Dudley et al. (SAND94-1884) LS-2 experimental dataset, and relevant receiver-scale and enhanced-geometry trends from the literature.
2. Quantify the effect of promoter twist pitch, promoter size, and promoter wall thickness on Nusselt number, friction factor, and performance evaluation criterion (PEC), both with the base fluid and with hybrid nanofluids.
3. Screen the three candidate hybrid nanofluids and their nanoparticle volume concentrations to identify the combination offering the most favorable thermal-hydraulic and thermodynamic performance.
4. Conduct energy, exergy, and entropy-generation analyses — including thermal efficiency, exergetic efficiency, total entropy generation, and Bejan number — to evaluate the combined geometry-and-nanofluid enhancement against the baseline smooth receiver.
5. Establish the optimal combination of promoter geometry and hybrid nanofluid configuration within the investigated range.

## 1.8 Thesis Outline

This thesis is organized into five chapters. **Chapter 1: Introduction** presents the background of the study, introduces solar collectors and parabolic trough solar collectors, discusses working fluids and passive heat-transfer enhancement techniques, and defines the research problem, scope, and objectives. **Chapter 2: Literature Review** reviews previous studies related to heat-transfer enhancement in PTSC systems, covering passive enhancement techniques, advanced heat-transfer fluids, and combined enhancement approaches, and identifies the research gap motivating the present study. **Chapter 3: Methodology** describes the numerical framework adopted in this research, including the physical model, governing equations, optical and thermal boundary conditions, CFD code structure, mesh generation and grid-independence study, working-fluid and hybrid-nanofluid property models, and model validation. **Chapter 4: Results and Discussion** presents and analyzes the numerical findings, covering the effects of promoter geometry, the screening and optimization of hybrid nanofluids, and the comparative thermal, hydraulic, and thermodynamic assessment of the smooth-tube, enhanced-geometry, and enhanced-geometry-with-nanofluid configurations. **Chapter 5: Conclusions and Recommendations** summarizes the main findings of the research, highlights its contribution, and provides recommendations for future work.
